Why My Heels Feel Like Concrete Every Morning — And What I Do About It

Some mornings I swing my legs off the bed and immediately wince — that sharp tug at the back of my ankle that reminds me I spent yesterday doing standing assessments for six hours straight. I've been a physiotherapist for over twenty years, and I still get tight heel cords. It's oddly reassuring, actually, because it means the stretches I recommend to my patients are things I genuinely need myself.

What's Actually Happening Back There

Your gastrocnemius muscle runs down the back of your calf and connects via the Achilles tendon to your heel bone. During sleep, your foot naturally points slightly downward, shortening that whole chain for hours. When you stand up, the tissue hasn't had time to ease back to its working length. That morning pull is simply your calf saying it needs a moment.

The Standing Wall Stretch I Do Before Coffee

I place both hands flat on the wall, step one foot back about 70 centimetres, keep that back knee completely straight, and press the heel firmly into the floor. I hold for 30 seconds, breathe slowly, and repeat three times per side. The key most people miss: the back leg must stay straight to target the gastrocnemius specifically — bend the knee and you shift the stretch down to the soleus instead. Both muscles matter, but start with the gastrocnemius because it's usually the tighter one in the morning.

The Step-Edge Stretch for a Deeper Pull

Once I've done the wall version, I move to my staircase and stand on the edge of the bottom step with my heels hanging off. I lower one heel slowly, hold for 20 seconds, come back to neutral, and switch sides. This adds a gravitational component that the wall stretch doesn't provide. I personally prefer this one for stubborn stiffness that doesn't shift after the wall version.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should I hold each gastrocnemius stretch?

I recommend 30 seconds per repetition, three rounds per side, for a total of about six minutes. Shorter holds under 15 seconds tend not to give the tissue enough time to respond — patience really does make a difference here.

Is it normal for the stretch to feel painful?

A strong pulling sensation is expected and fine. Sharp, stabbing, or burning pain is not — if you experience that, stop immediately and see a physiotherapist or doctor before continuing any stretch routine.
