That Deep Ache When You Sit Too Long — What My Piriformis Was Trying to Tell Me

Last winter, I spent three hours on a bench during a youth tournament, and by the time I stood up, I felt this sharp, drilling sensation deep in my right buttock — not the back, not the hamstring, but somewhere buried underneath both. I'd heard of the piriformis muscle before, mostly from physios talking about runners. But sitting? I never connected the two until that day in the cold Madrid stands.

What Actually Is the Piriformis and Why Does Sitting Upset It

The piriformis is a small, pear-shaped muscle sitting deep inside the gluteal region, running from the sacrum to the top of the femur. When you sit for extended periods — especially on hard surfaces or with poor posture — it can tighten and compress the sciatic nerve nearby. That compression creates a very specific kind of discomfort: deep, dull, sometimes shooting down the leg. It's not dramatic at first, but ignore it long enough and it starts affecting how you walk, stand, and coach from the sideline.

The Stretches That Actually Helped Me

The figure-four stretch became my daily ritual — lying on my back, crossing one ankle over the opposite knee, and gently pulling the uncrossed leg toward my chest. I hold it for 40 to 60 seconds per side, twice a day, no rushing. The seated piriformis stretch also works well when I'm at my desk between blog edits: sit upright, place one ankle on the opposite thigh, then slowly lean forward. My personal opinion: consistency beats intensity every single time with this muscle — doing it gently every day outperforms one aggressive session per week.

Adding Warmth Before the Stretch

One thing I added to my routine about six months ago was applying a warming massage cream to the gluteal area before stretching. Warming the tissue manually before movement just makes sense mechanically — muscle that's been warmed responds differently than cold, stiff tissue. I use a small circular motion to work the cream in for two to three minutes before I even attempt the figure-four position. The ritual of it also slows me down, which honestly is half the benefit for a restless coach like me.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take before piriformis stretches make a noticeable difference?

Most people notice some change in tension levels within two to three weeks of daily stretching, but it really depends on how long the tightness has been building. Don't expect overnight results — this muscle responds to patience, not force.

Can I do piriformis stretches if I also have lower back issues?

It's worth checking with a physiotherapist first, since some lower back conditions change which positions are safe for you. That said, gentle versions of the figure-four stretch are generally well tolerated by most people.
