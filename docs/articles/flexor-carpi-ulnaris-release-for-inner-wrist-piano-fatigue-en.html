My Inner Wrist Aches After Every Piano Session — Here's What I Started Doing

Last Tuesday I watched my student Marco walk off the piano bench holding his inner wrist like it had personally offended him. I knew that look. Years ago, after a particularly long restorative yoga sequence heavy on forearm weight-bearing, I felt that exact ache — deep, dull, running along the pinky side of the wrist. It's the flexor carpi ulnaris muscle, and pianists push it hard without even realizing it.

What Is the Flexor Carpi Ulnaris and Why Pianists Feel It

The flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU) runs along the ulnar side of your forearm and anchors right at the wrist. Every octave stretch, every repeated bass note pattern, every sustained chord tightens it incrementally. Pianists often don't notice tension building until they stand up from the bench. By then the muscle is already holding a lot.

The Release Technique I Use After Playing

I start by sitting comfortably and resting my forearm palm-up on a rolled towel. Using the thumb of my opposite hand, I apply slow, deliberate pressure along the FCU line from just above the wrist up toward the elbow — no rushing. I pause at any point that feels dense or tender for about 10 seconds. This takes maybe four minutes total, but it genuinely changes how my wrist feels afterward.

Adding Massage Cream to the Routine

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know it's the FCU and not something else?

The FCU sits along the inner (pinky) side of the forearm. If your ache is on that side — not the thumb side — and gets more noticeable after repetitive wrist flexion like piano playing, it's a reasonable guess. That said, persistent or sharp pain should always be looked at by a professional.

Can I do this release every day?

I do it most days I practice and haven't had any issues. The key is keeping pressure firm but not aggressive — you're working with tissue, not against it. If anything feels worse rather than better after a session, take a day off and reassess.
