That Nagging Ache on Top of Your Shoulder? Here's How a Simple Supraspinatus Massage Can Help You Feel More Like Yourself Again

If you spend most of your day hunched over a desk, reaching overhead, or just carrying more tension than you'd like to admit, that dull, persistent ache right on top of your shoulder probably sounds familiar. It often comes from the supraspinatus — a small but hardworking muscle that sits along the top of your shoulder blade and takes a beating from daily life. The good news is that a focused, intentional massage of this area can make a real difference in how you feel day to day.

What Exactly Is the Supraspinatus and Why Does It Get So Tight?

The supraspinatus is one of the four rotator cuff muscles, and it runs horizontally across the top of your shoulder blade, tucking under the bony ridge you can feel at the top of your shoulder. It helps lift your arm out to the side and keeps your shoulder joint stable during almost every arm movement you make. Because of how much it works — and how often we hold tension in our upper shoulders without realizing it — this muscle can become tight, knotted, and just plain unhappy. Things like sitting with rounded shoulders, carrying bags on one side, sleeping in awkward positions, or doing repetitive overhead tasks all contribute to that familiar top-of-shoulder soreness. When the supraspinatus gets overworked and under-stretched, the tissue starts to feel dense and tender, and that ache can spread up into your neck or down through your upper arm if you ignore it long enough.

How to Actually Massage the Supraspinatus Yourself

Self-massaging the supraspinatus takes a little patience because it sits in a tricky spot, but once you find it, the technique is surprisingly simple. Start by reaching your opposite hand across your chest and draping it over the top of your shoulder — your fingertips should land somewhere around the base of your neck, right where the muscle belly sits. You're looking for a ridge of tissue that feels rope-like or noticeably tender when pressed. Begin with broad, slow circular strokes using your fingertips to warm up the area. Then, once the tissue feels a little more receptive, switch to slow, firm stripping strokes — dragging your fingers from the base of your neck outward toward the top of your arm in one long, deliberate motion. Do this for two to three minutes at a time, keeping your breathing slow and steady. If a spot feels especially tight, pause on it and apply gentle sustained pressure for ten to fifteen seconds before moving on. You can also use a tennis ball against a wall if your hands tire easily — position it right at the top of your shoulder and gently lean into it, letting gravity do the work.
