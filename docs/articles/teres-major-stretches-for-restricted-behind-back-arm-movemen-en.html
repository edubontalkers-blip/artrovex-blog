Can't Reach Behind Your Back? Your Teres Major Might Be the Culprit

You go to tuck in your shirt, buckle a back strap, or scratch between your shoulder blades — and your arm just won't cooperate. That tight, stubborn wall of resistance you hit is a frustrating but very common problem. Nine times out of ten, the teres major muscle is a big part of why that behind-back movement feels so locked down.

What Is the Teres Major and Why Does It Get So Tight?

The teres major is a small but powerful muscle that runs along the back of your shoulder blade down to the upper arm bone. It helps pull your arm inward and backward, which sounds useful — and it is — but when it gets chronically shortened from lots of desk work, overhead lifting, sleeping on one side, or just general stress tension, it starts to fight against the opposite movements. Reaching behind your back requires your arm to rotate outward and move into internal extension, which is basically the opposite of what a tight teres major wants to do. So it pulls back, you feel a wall, and the movement stops short. The good news is that this muscle responds really well to targeted stretching when you're consistent about it.

Three Teres Major Stretches Worth Doing Every Day

Start with the doorway overhead stretch. Stand in a doorway and raise the arm you want to stretch straight up, placing the back of your wrist against the door frame near the top. Gently lean your body slightly forward and away from that arm until you feel a deep pull along the back and side of your shoulder. Hold for 30 seconds and breathe slowly. Next, try the cross-body pulldown stretch. Sit or stand tall, raise your arm straight up, then bend the elbow so your hand drops behind your head. Use your other hand to gently hold the bent elbow and draw it slightly across toward the opposite ear — not forcefully, just a gentle guided pull. You should feel this deep in the back of the armpit area where the teres major lives. Hold 30 to 45 seconds per side. The third one is the sleeper stretch variation, done lying on your side. Lie on the floor on the side you want to stretch, with your arm out in front of you at shoulder height and elbow bent at 90 degrees. Use your other hand to slowly press your forearm toward the floor. This gets into both the teres major and the surrounding rotator cuff area. Go slowly — there is no rushing this one. Three rounds of each stretch, done daily, can genuinely shift how that behind-back movement feels over a few weeks.
