Why Your Outer Forearm Won't Stop Twisting Tight — And What I Do About It

Last winter I was demoing a backhand drill with one of my junior athletes and my right forearm locked up mid-rotation — not painful exactly, but that deep, stubborn tightness that makes you want to shake your arm like a wet dog. I've had it on and off since my late thirties. Being 48 and still coaching six days a week means I can't ignore these things the way I used to.

What the Supinator Actually Does (And Why It Gets So Tight)

The supinator is a deep forearm muscle that rotates your palm upward — every time you open a door, swing a racket, or lift a bag, it fires. Most people have never heard of it. That's exactly why it gets ignored until it becomes a nagging source of tightness along the outer forearm and elbow. In my experience coaching athletes of all levels, this muscle is chronically undertreated.

My Go-To Release Technique

I start with a cross-fiber friction: thumb pressed firmly just below the lateral epicondyle, moving slowly across the muscle belly rather than along it. Then I move into a pin-and-stretch — press the tissue down with your opposite thumb, and slowly pronate your forearm (rotate palm downward) while holding that pressure. Do this for about 60 to 90 seconds per spot. It's not glamorous, but it works.

Where Most People Go Wrong

They rush it. They spend 20 seconds on the area, feel nothing, and give up. The supinator sits deep under the brachioradialis, so surface rubbing does almost nothing. You need sustained, targeted pressure combined with active movement. My personal opinion: most forearm tightness people blame on tennis elbow actually originates here, in the supinator, and it gets missed entirely.

How often should I do supinator release work?

I do it 3 to 4 times per week when tightness is present, and once a week as maintenance. Daily is fine too — the tissue responds well to consistent, gentle attention rather than one aggressive session.

Absolutely. Desk workers, drivers, and anyone who types a lot can develop supinator tightness from repetitive low-load rotation. You don't need an athletic history for this muscle to cause problems.
