Why My Elbow Ached After Every Long Hike — And What I Do About It Now

Last October, coming down from the Karwendel, my right elbow felt like someone had packed wet sand inside the joint. I kept bending it — trekking poles, backpack straps, scrambling sections — and by kilometre 18 it was just done. That moment was when I started paying serious attention to the brachialis, a muscle most hikers have never even heard of.

What Is the Brachialis and Why Does Hiking Wreck It?

The brachialis sits underneath the biceps and is actually the primary elbow flexor — it works every single time you bend your arm past about 90 degrees. On a long descent with poles, or scrambling over boulders, it fires constantly. Unlike the biceps, it gets almost zero stretching love during a normal cool-down. That accumulated fatigue builds up as a dense, heavy ache right in the anterior elbow crease.

How I Massage the Brachialis After a Big Day Out

I sit down, rest my forearm on my thigh with the elbow at roughly 70 degrees — that position softens the muscle slightly. Then I use my opposite thumb to sink into the tissue just medial to the biceps tendon, working in slow, deliberate circles toward the humerus. Short strokes, not gliding. I spend about four minutes per side, which honestly feels like forever but makes a noticeable difference by morning.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I massage the brachialis myself or do I need a partner?

Self-massage works well here — the position I described (forearm resting on thigh, elbow slightly bent) gives you good access and enough leverage with your opposite thumb. A partner can go deeper, but honestly the self-massage is something you can do in your tent or hotel room immediately after a hike.

How soon after hiking should I do this massage?

I wait until I have showered and the skin is clean and warm — usually within an hour of finishing. Doing it while the muscle is still in a fatigued state seems to help me recover faster overnight, though everyone is different.

Is Hondrocream safe to use on the elbow crease area where skin is thin?

I use it there regularly without any issue — I just use less product than on larger muscle groups and avoid the very inside of the crease where the ulnar nerve sits close to the surface. If you have sensitive skin, do a small patch test on your forearm first, which is general good practice with any warming cream.
