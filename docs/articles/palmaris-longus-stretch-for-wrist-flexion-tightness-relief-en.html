The Palmaris Longus Stretch That Finally Sorted My Wrist Tightness After Years of Training

Last winter I was typing match notes at my desk and noticed my right wrist felt like it had a rubber band wrapped around the inside of it. I am 48, I played rugby for fifteen years, and I have coached for another decade after that — tight wrists are basically an old friend I never wanted. That specific pulling sensation along the inner forearm sent me back to basics: the palmaris longus stretch.

What Is the Palmaris Longus and Why Does It Get So Tight?

The palmaris longus is a slender tendon running from your inner elbow down through the center of your wrist, and about 15% of people do not even have one — lucky them. For the rest of us, repetitive gripping, keyboard use, or years of sport can leave it feeling short and stubborn. You can spot it yourself: press your thumb to your little finger and flex your wrist slightly — if a thin cord pops up in the middle of your forearm, that is yours. It is a small structure with an outsized ability to contribute to that deep inner-wrist tightness.

How to Do the Palmaris Longus Stretch Properly

Sit or stand comfortably, extend one arm straight in front of you with the palm facing up. Use your other hand to gently bend the wrist backward — fingers pointing down toward the floor — until you feel a mild pull along the inner forearm, not pain. Hold that position for 20 to 30 seconds, breathe normally, and repeat three times per side. My personal opinion: most people rush this stretch and hold for five seconds, which accomplishes almost nothing — the tissue needs time to respond.

Building It Into Your Daily Routine Without Overthinking It

I do this stretch every morning before I even pick up my coffee, and again in the evening after I have been writing or on the laptop for hours. Pairing it with a short forearm self-massage afterward makes a noticeable difference in how the wrist moves the next morning. You do not need a full session — three minutes of focused attention on that inner forearm goes further than most people expect. Consistency over intensity is the only principle that has ever actually worked for me across 25 years of body maintenance.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long before I notice a difference from doing this stretch regularly?

Most people I coach report that the inner wrist feels noticeably more mobile after five to seven days of consistent daily practice — but it really depends on how long the tightness has been building up. If it has been years, give it three to four weeks before judging.

Can I do this stretch if my wrist already clicks or makes noise?

Clicking alone is usually not a reason to stop gentle stretching, but if there is sharp pain at any point during the movement, stop and get the wrist looked at by a physiotherapist before continuing. This stretch should feel like a mild pull, nothing sharper than that.

Is Artrovex cream safe to use every day on the forearm?
