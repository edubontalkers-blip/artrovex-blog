Why Your Deep Sitting Discomfort Might Be Coming From a Muscle You've Never Heard Of

Last winter I spent three hours on a train from Rome to Milan, and by the time I arrived I could barely walk without that familiar deep ache right in the centre of my pelvis. I'd felt it before during my athletic career, but back then I just pushed through. Now, at 48, I actually pay attention to what my body is telling me.

What Is the Obturator Internus and Why Does It Get So Tight?

The obturator internus is a small, deep muscle that wraps around the inside of your pelvis and attaches near the hip joint. It works hard during rotation and stabilisation movements — things you probably don't think about until they stop working well. When you sit for long periods, especially on hard or low surfaces, this muscle can shorten and stay compressed. The result is that dull, hard-to-describe pressure deep in the pelvic floor area that many people mistake for a hip or lower back problem.

How I Release It — My Actual Routine

I use a firm massage ball — a lacrosse ball works perfectly — placed just beside the sit bone while I'm seated on a sturdy chair. I shift my weight slowly onto the ball and hold for 30 to 60 seconds, breathing steadily. Then I add a slow figure-four stretch lying on my back, pulling the knee gently across. I repeat this sequence twice, and honestly, after years of coaching athletes, this is the one drill I wish I had known in my twenties.

Warming the Tissue Before You Start Makes a Real Difference

Cold, tight muscle tissue does not release easily — anyone who has trained seriously knows this. Before I begin the release work, I apply a warming massage cream directly over the gluteal area and the lateral hip. The warmth helps the tissue feel more responsive, and the act of massaging it in also gives me a moment to feel where the tension actually sits. It's a simple step that costs almost nothing in time but changes the whole quality of the session.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my discomfort is actually the obturator internus and not the piriformis?

Both muscles live in a similar neighbourhood, so the sensations can overlap. The obturator internus tends to create discomfort more centrally and deeper in the pelvic floor area, while piriformis tension usually shows up more toward the outer buttock. That said, a manual therapist can pinpoint it far better than any self-diagnosis — worth a visit if you're unsure.

Can I do this release every day?

I personally do it three to four times a week, not every single day. Deep muscle tissue needs time to adapt between sessions, especially when you're first starting out. If you feel increased soreness the day after, give yourself an extra rest day.
