Last winter I reached for a water bottle on the top shelf and my right shoulder simply refused. Not painful exactly — just locked, like a rusty gate. I'd been coaching football in Madrid for over twenty years and never paid attention to that deep muscle sitting behind my shoulder blade. After my knee injury forced me to slow down and actually learn anatomy, the infraspinatus became the muscle I wish I'd known about decades earlier.

What the Infraspinatus Actually Does (And Why It Stiffens Up)

The infraspinatus is one of the four rotator cuff muscles, and its main job is external rotation — that motion you make when you open a door or throw a ball sideways. When it tightens, reaching across your body or lifting your arm outward starts to feel like pulling a bungee cord. In athletes and coaches alike, years of repetitive motion without proper recovery work can leave this muscle chronically shortened. I say this not as a doctor but as someone who spent three months wondering why my shoulder felt ten years older than the rest of me.

The Massage Approach I Use — Step by Step

Sit in a chair and let your arm hang relaxed — tension in the arm makes the infraspinatus harder to reach properly. Use your opposite hand's fingers or a massage ball to press into the area just below the spine of your shoulder blade, working slowly in small circles. I spend about sixty to ninety seconds on each tender spot before moving a centimetre further along the muscle. My personal opinion: patience here matters more than pressure — grinding hard into the tissue accomplishes nothing good.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long before I notice my shoulder rotating more freely?

That depends entirely on how long the stiffness has been building. Some people feel a difference in range after two or three consistent sessions; others need several weeks of regular work. I noticed meaningful improvement after about twelve days of daily massage combined with gentle rotation stretches.

Can I use a massage ball instead of my fingers on the infraspinatus?

Absolutely — a lacrosse ball or firm rubber ball pressed between your back and a wall works very well for reaching this muscle without tiring your hand. Position the ball just below the shoulder blade spine and move your body slowly to control the pressure. I actually prefer this method for days when my hands are tired from coaching drills.

Is it normal for the infraspinatus area to feel bruised after massage?

Mild soreness the next day is common, especially if the muscle hasn't been worked before. That said, sharp pain during the massage or bruising visible on the skin means you're pressing too hard or in the wrong spot — ease off and use lighter pressure. I always treat tenderness as a signal to adjust, not push through.

If your shoulder has been silently locking you out of movements you used to take for granted, start with five minutes of infraspinatus work tonight — your future self will raise a fully rotating arm in thanks.
