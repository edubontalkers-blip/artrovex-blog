That Aching Inner Shin After a Long Run — Here's What I Do

Last October, after a 22-kilometer run along the Spree, I sat on my apartment floor at midnight pressing my thumb into the inside of my shin like it personally owed me something. That deep, nagging ache wasn't my calf. It was my tibialis posterior — a muscle most runners never even hear about until it starts complaining. I've been a personal trainer for over a decade, and this one still catches people off guard.

What Is the Tibialis Posterior and Why Should Runners Care

The tibialis posterior runs deep inside the lower leg, attaching from the back of the tibia and fibula down to the inner arch of the foot. It controls how your foot pronates with every single step — so over long distances, it works constantly. When it gets overloaded, you feel it as a dull, sometimes sharp ache along the inner shin. Most people blame their shoes first. I always check this muscle first.

How I Actually Massage It — The Technique I Use on Myself and Clients

Sit on the floor with one knee bent, foot flat. Use your thumb or a massage ball to press into the inner edge of the shin, just behind the tibia bone — not on it, behind it. Work slowly in short strokes from mid-shin downward toward the ankle, pausing on tender spots for five to ten seconds. It will feel uncomfortable at first; that's normal, and it should ease within a few seconds of steady pressure.

When to Add Massage and When to Back Off

I use this massage on rest days or light training days, never directly after a hard session when tissue is already inflamed and sensitive. If you're feeling this ache consistently for more than two weeks, please get eyes on it from a physio — I say that as someone who waited too long once and regretted it. Self-massage is a recovery support tool, not a substitute for professional assessment. My personal opinion: runners underestimate how much consistent, low-intensity maintenance work can change how a body holds up over months.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I do this massage every day?

I wouldn't go every single day — two to four times per week on non-intense training days is a reasonable rhythm. Your tissue needs time to respond and recover between sessions.

How do I know it's the tibialis posterior and not a stress fracture?

Stress fractures usually produce sharp, pinpoint pain directly on the bone that gets worse with impact — not the same as the broader ache behind the shin. That said, if you're unsure, see a physio or doctor. Please don't guess on bone pain.

Is Artrovex cream safe for everyday skin?

It's a cosmetic massage cream, so it's designed for topical skin use during massage — always check the ingredient list if you have known sensitivities and patch test first if you're trying it for the first time.
