Why Your Shoulders Feel Stuck Up by Your Ears (And What to Do About It)

If you spend most of your day hunched over a keyboard or constantly looking down at your phone, there is a good chance your shoulders have quietly crept upward and decided to stay there. That tight, pulled-forward feeling is something a lot of people just accept as normal — but it does not have to be. Stretching the pectoral minor, a small but surprisingly stubborn muscle tucked under your chest, can make a real difference in how your whole upper body feels and moves.

What Is the Pectoral Minor and Why Does It Pull Your Shoulders Up?

The pectoral minor is a thin, triangular muscle that sits beneath the larger pectoral major — the one most people think of when they say chest muscle. It runs from your ribs up to a bony point on your shoulder blade called the coracoid process. When this muscle gets tight, which happens easily from sitting, driving, or any forward-reaching posture, it tugs the front of your shoulder blade downward and tips the top of your shoulder forward and upward. The result is that classic rounded, elevated shoulder posture that feels almost impossible to shake. You try to roll your shoulders back and they just bounce right back to where they were. The reason is that the pectoral minor has shortened over time and needs to be gently, consistently stretched back to a comfortable resting length.

The Best Pectoral Minor Stretches to Add to Your Daily Routine

You do not need a gym or any equipment for these. The doorway stretch is the most accessible option: stand in a doorway, place your forearm flat against the frame with your elbow at roughly shoulder height or slightly below, then gently step through the doorway until you feel a stretch across the front of your chest and shoulder. Hold for 30 seconds, breathe steadily, and repeat on both sides. For a more targeted version, lower your elbow to about a 90-degree angle at your side — this hits the pectoral minor more directly than the higher arm position. Another great option is the corner stretch, where you stand facing a corner and place both forearms on the walls, then lean your body forward gently. If you want something you can do at your desk, try the hands-behind-back squeeze: clasp your hands behind your lower back, straighten your arms, and gently lift them while opening your chest toward the ceiling. These are not complicated movements, but consistency is everything. Doing them once is not going to undo months of tightness. Doing them daily, even just for a few minutes, adds up quickly.
