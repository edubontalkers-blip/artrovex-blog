Why the Top of Your Foot Gets Tight When You Walk — And What I Do About It

Last autumn, a woman came into my studio barely able to lift her toes off the ground after a two-week hiking trip through the Ardèche. She wasn't injured — she was just incredibly tight along the top of her foot, right along that long tendon that runs from the shin down to the big toe. That muscle, the extensor hallucis longus, gets ignored constantly, and I think that's a mistake.

What the Extensor Hallucis Longus Actually Does

This muscle pulls your big toe upward and helps lift the front of your foot with every step. When you walk a lot — especially on uneven terrain, in stiff shoes, or going uphill — it works overtime without any credit. Most people feel the tightness as a dull ache or a pulling sensation on the top of the foot, sometimes running toward the ankle. It's not dramatic, but it is persistent.

How I Work This Area on Clients

I start by locating the tendon — it's visible when you flex your big toe upward, running like a cord from mid-foot toward the shin. I use slow, longitudinal strokes along the tendon with moderate pressure, never pressing directly onto the bone. Then I work the muscle belly higher up the shin in small cross-fiber movements. The whole sequence takes maybe eight minutes, but clients always comment on how much lighter their foot feels afterward.

The Double Application Tip That Makes a Difference

For this particular area, I always apply the cream twice — once at the start to prepare the tissue, and a second time midway through when the skin has absorbed the first layer. The second pass lets me work a little deeper without dragging. It sounds simple, but skipping that second application genuinely changes the quality of the work, especially on clients with drier skin or very tight fascia.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I do this massage on myself at home?

Yes, absolutely — sit with your foot propped on your opposite knee, flex your big toe to find the tendon, and use your thumb to stroke slowly along it toward your shin. It won't be identical to a professional session, but it's genuinely useful for regular maintenance.

How often should I work this area if I walk a lot daily?

In my experience, two to three times a week is a reasonable rhythm for active people — daily walking creates ongoing tension, so regular attention keeps it from building up into something more stubborn.
